We Tested 23 AI Writing Tools on Real Client Work. Only 5 Survived.

23 tools, four real client briefs, three weeks, $300 of my own money. Five survived. Here's the honest ranking — including the tool that framed my cold email.

MKBy Mara Kessler·Jul 28, 2026·18 min read

Tuesday, 11:47pm. I was on my third rewrite of an intro a $900 client had already rejected twice, and the fourth writing tool of the day had just handed me back my own sentence wearing a thesaurus costume. That's when I decided to run this properly.

Three weeks, a little over $300 of my own money, 23 writing assistants, four identical briefs: a 1,200-word blog post for a SaaS client in Denver (she lets me use the brief, not the name), a three-email cold sequence, a batch of twelve product descriptions, and a 3,000-word research piece on sleep supplements — a topic, if you're wondering, that is absolutely lousy with half-true claims.

Five tools made the cut. Eighteen didn't. Some of the rejects were honestly funny. One rewrote my cold email so hard the opener became "In today's fast-paced business landscape." I framed it.

How we tested (the boring, honest part)

Four briefs, four axes: did a human editor still have to fix it, how fast until a usable draft, how often did it invent facts, and what did a useful word actually cost after the subscription math. I paid for everything myself, and no tool knew it was being compared, because a tool that knows it's in a review behaves differently.

The separator between the top two and the rest wasn't polish. It was lying. I logged every invented fact by hand — 61 total across the whole run — and the distribution was not close.

Pretty prose that lies is a liability. Ugly prose that's true is a Tuesday.

The rankings

1Jasper9.4

Jasper is the only tool here that survived contact with a real brand book. I fed it three samples of my Denver client's voice — dry, slightly allergic to buzzwords — and by draft two it had stopped opening paragraphs with "Unlock." Not perfect. But eerily close to her cadence, including her habit of ending on a short fragment.

Where it fell apart: the research piece. Jasper kept trying to tie a bow on a topic that doesn't have one. I watched it invent a "consensus" that doesn't exist, twice, in the same paragraph. You will fact-check Jasper. Budget for that.

What we loved

  • Brand-voice memory is real, not a demo trick
  • Workflows match how content teams actually run
  • Extension catches you in every text box

What hurt

  • Long-form research needs a leash
  • $39 stings when you're solo
  • Defaults to marketing-speak if you blink
Pricing: Starts at $39/mo (annual) · 7-day free trial
2Claude (Anthropic)9.2

Not a "writing tool," whatever that means now. I pasted the whole style guide, two competitor pieces, and the brief — 31,000 words of context — and it held all of it. The prose has a rhythm I genuinely struggle to separate from a good human freelancer's. It also lied the least: two invented facts across the entire run, both soft (a date, a rounding).

The catch is there's no product around it. No SEO suggestions, no templates, no team seats. You are the workflow. For some people that's the point.

What we loved

  • Best raw prose on this list, by ear
  • Huge context window swallows your whole style guide
  • Hallucinated the least in my fact log

What hurt

  • No SEO or workflow tooling at all
  • Brand memory is DIY between chats
  • Free-tier rate limits arrive fast
Pricing: $20/mo Pro · generous free tier
3Sudowrite8.8

If you write fiction, stop reading this list and go try the Story Engine. It understands scene structure in a way that felt, twice, like it had read my outline better than I had. My tester — my niece, 19, writes fanfic, brutally honest — said the Describe feature "writes the sentence I was too lazy to write." From her, that's a wedding speech.

For marketing copy it's the wrong animal. No SEO, no bulk mode, and pricing that assumes you're an author with an advance, not a blogger with a side hustle.

What we loved

  • Best-in-class for fiction and narrative
  • Story Engine actually understands plot
  • Feels like a co-writer, not a slot machine

What hurt

  • Wrong tool for marketing/SEO copy
  • Steep for hobbyists
  • Full feature set has a learning curve
Pricing: From $10/mo · free trial available
4Grammarly8.5

Grammarly doesn't belong on a "generators" list and I don't care. Every draft from every tool above still needed a human pass, and at 11pm (see opening) the thing that catches the doubled "the" and the comma that flips a contract sentence is Grammarly. It saved me from shipping "we help clients kill their churn" versus "we help clients, kill their churn." Read those two aloud. You're welcome.

It won't write for you. It will make sure what you ship isn't embarrassing, which at midnight is the same thing.

What we loved

  • Catches what every other tool misses
  • Tone detection is quietly excellent
  • Lives in every browser text box

What hurt

  • Generates nothing
  • Premium adds up over a year
  • Over-corrects intentional style now and then
Pricing: $12/mo (annual) · free tier is solid
5Copy.ai8.2

The cheap one that doesn't feel cheap until week three. First two weeks are great: enormous template library, bulk descriptions that actually vary their sentence openers. Then you notice the voice drifting — paragraph one confident, paragraph nine a different, sleepier writer. On high-volume, low-stakes copy (product feeds, internal docs) the drift never matters. On a brand, it's the whole ballgame.

What we loved

  • Template library is genuinely enormous
  • Bulk workflows punch above the price
  • Best price-to-output ratio I tested

What hurt

  • Voice drifts on long projects
  • Support answers on geological time
  • UI gets noisy after a while
Pricing: Free plan available · Pro from $36/mo

The verdict

Broke? Claude free + Grammarly free. That combo carried me through 2024 and would carry a student today.

Client work where the voice is the product? Jasper, and eat the $39.

Fiction? Sudowrite, and ignore this entire site except that sentence.

And a rule I've now said out loud to three clients: don't buy a subscription for a tool you'll use twice a month. The free tiers in 2026 are good enough that "can't afford it" is almost never the real bottleneck. Attention is.

FAQ

Will Google penalize AI-written content?
Google penalizes useless content, not provenance. I've watched AI-assisted pieces rank #1 and hand-written garbage rank nowhere in the same month. Write for a reader in a hurry and the question answers itself.
Can these tools pass AI detectors?
Sometimes, and it matters less than the detector industry wants you to think. False positives are everywhere — my niece's entirely human fanfiction got flagged by two detectors, which taught her a lot about detectors and nothing about her writing. Edit in your own voice anyway; better writing and quieter detector scores come from the same habit.
Is Jasper worth $39 when Claude costs $20?
For one specific buyer, yes: someone billing clients under a single brand voice every week, where the voice memory pays for itself in saved editing time. For everyone else the $20 generalist wins — and I say that as the person who still has the Jasper receipt.
