Best AI Grammar Checkers 2026: Ranked by What They Actually Catch

Eight checkers, one shared promise — "never send something embarrassing." Here's the framework for matching the tool to what you write, where you write it, and what you're willing to pay for the catches you need.

MKBy Mara Kessler·Aug 27, 2026·9 min read
How to read this review: this is a comparison framework, not a lab report. Scores are editorial judgment built from official docs and public information as of August 2026; prices are flagged where they still need verification; anecdotes are illustrative. Full method on the About page.

My friend Rico the plumber posted his first job ad this spring, and his niece read it over his shoulder before it went live. She found three things: "definitely" spelled the way it sounds, a sentence that accidentally promised free boilers, and a comma situation she described only as "a crime." The ad got fixed, the apprentices applied — and Rico asked me which of these grammar apps is "the one," as if there were one.

There isn't. The honest problem with grammar checkers is that they catch different things in different places: one is brilliant in your browser but absent from your Word file, one fixes commas but can't hear tone, one handles French beautifully and English-only tools can't touch it. So this piece is a comparison framework — what each tool actually catches, where it lives, what free really means, and what the meter does when free runs out.

One boundary first: these are writing assistants — grammar, spelling, clarity, tone. If you want AI to draft whole documents from scratch, that's the writing-tools list; if you want text moved between languages, that's the translation list. Everything below assumes the words are yours and the embarrassment is the risk.

How this comparison works (the honest version)

Five questions, one line each, so you know my ruler before you see my scores. Catch quality: does it spot real grammar errors, or mostly shuffle your commas around? Reach: browser extension, Word add-in, phone keyboard, desktop app — a checker that isn't where you write is a checker you don't use. Free-tier honesty: is free a working tool or a teaser with a paywall every third suggestion? Privacy: your writing goes to their servers, so who sees it and what the terms say matters more here than in most categories. Price: subscription shape, annual-versus-monthly spread, and whether the jump to paid actually buys catches or just removes limits.

Scores out of ten, editorial judgment, standing on an honest ruler rather than a benchmark nobody ran. Prices in this category change with promo seasons, so everything carries a "verify current" flag.

A grammar checker is a seatbelt, not a driver. It catches the crash you didn't see; it doesn't decide where you're going.

The lineup

1Grammarly9.0

The name on the category for a reason: Grammarly's corrections are the most polished here, its explanations actually teach, and it lives nearly everywhere — browser, Word, desktop, phone keyboard. The paid tier (renamed Pro) adds the things people actually renew for: full-sentence rewrites, tone adjustments, plagiarism checking against web sources, and style rules you can set per document. Free is a real tool, not a demo: core grammar, spelling and punctuation, no card required.

The honest limits: it's English-only, its suggestions occasionally sand the personality out of deliberate prose, and the pricing spread is wide — Pro runs about $12 a month when billed annually (roughly $144 a year) but the month-to-month price is dramatically higher, near $30 (verify current). If you write in English daily and the stakes are professional, it's still the default answer to Rico's question.

What we loved

  • Most polished corrections and explanations
  • Lives in browser, Word, phone
  • Free tier is a real tool

What hurt

  • English-only
  • Can flatten deliberate style
  • Monthly price far above annual
Pricing: Free · Pro ~$12/mo billed annually (~$144/yr) · ~$30/mo monthly (verify current)
2LanguageTool8.6

The multilingual champion — and that's not a participation trophy. LanguageTool checks grammar and style across 25+ languages, so if your week mixes English, French, German or Spanish, one extension covers the whole map where Grammarly can't follow at all. It lives in browsers, Word and as a stand-alone editor, and the free tier works without even an account, which is rarer than it should be.

Its English suggestions are solid but a step less sharp than Grammarly's best catches, and the free tier has character caps per check that heavy writers will hit (published figures for the cap differ across sources — verify on the site). Premium has been reported around $59.90 a year with promotional pricing common (verify current). If your life happens in more than one language, this is the answer and there isn't really a second option.

What hurt

  • Promo pricing makes cost unclear
Pricing: Free (character caps) · Premium reported ~$59.90/yr with frequent promos (verify current)
3ProWritingAid8.4

The writing coach for people who write books. Where Grammarly fixes sentences, ProWritingAid interrogates manuscripts: repeated words, pacing, sticky sentences, overused adverbs, readability by chapter — twenty-odd reports that read like an editor's letter. For novelists and long-form nonfiction, no other tool here thinks at that altitude, and paid plans carry no word limits.

The honest trade-offs: it's English-only, the interface assumes you want homework with your corrections, and casual writers will feel buried. Pricing moved recently — the old cheap annual plan is gone; Premium now runs about $120 a year ($10 a month billed annually) or roughly $30 month-to-month, with a one-time lifetime option around €399 and a higher Premium Pro tier adding AI features (verify current). Buy it for the manuscript, not the email.

4QuillBot8.1

The free-tier king. QuillBot bundles grammar checking with a genuinely useful paraphraser, summarizer and citation machine, and the free plan gives you working versions of all of them — which is why students and budget-conscious writers land here first and stay longer than expected. When a sentence is grammatically fine but just wrong, its rephrase modes are often the faster fix than rewriting it yourself.

The meter is the catch: free paraphrasing caps at 125 words at a time and some tools get a few uses a day, so volume pushes you toward Premium — reported around $10 a month billed annually up to roughly $19.95 month-to-month, with constant discounts (verify current). Grammar detection itself is a step below Grammarly on subtle errors. Best framing: the best free kit in the category, with a paid ceiling.

What we loved

  • Most generous free bundle
  • Paraphrase and summarizer included
  • Citation machine for students

What hurt

  • Free caps at 125 words per pass
  • Grammar a step below Grammarly
  • Discount pricing is a moving target
Pricing: Free (word caps) · Premium ~$10/mo annual to ~$19.95 monthly (verify current)
5Wordtune7.8

The rewording specialist. Wordtune's core trick is offering several alternative phrasings for any sentence — shorter, longer, more formal, more casual — so you pick a voice instead of accepting a correction. For non-native English writers polishing emails, that menu of options teaches more than a red underline ever does, and the extensions work across most places you type.

It's a narrower tool than the giants: grammar checking exists but isn't the headline, the free tier allows only a handful of rewrites a day, and paid tiers have been reported from about $6.99 a month (annual) to around $9.99 for unlimited (verify current). Think of it as the seasoning shelf, not the kitchen — brilliant for making a sentence yours, not for catching every comma crime.

What we loved

  • Multiple rewrite options per sentence
  • Tone control: formal to casual
  • Great for non-native writers

What hurt

  • Grammar checking isn't the core
  • Free tier: few rewrites per day
  • Narrower than full suites
Pricing: Free (daily rewrites) · Advanced ~$6.99/mo · Unlimited ~$9.99/mo annual (verify current)
6DeepL Write7.7

The free phrase-fixer from the translation people. DeepL Write takes your sentence and returns cleaner, more natural versions — fixing errors along the way — with a tone dial for formal or casual registers. Because it's built by DeepL, the phrasing instincts are unusually good, and the whole thing is free at the moment, no account gymnastics required (verify current; free tools can grow paywalls).

The honest limits: it covers a small set of languages rather than the full DeepL roster, it works text-by-text rather than living inside every app you write in, and it's a rewriter more than a proofreader — you won't get a full-document audit. Use it as the last pass on important paragraphs, the thing you paste a client email into before sending, and it earns its zero price tag.

What we loved

  • Rewriter more than proofreader

7Microsoft Editor7.5

The checker your computer already has. Editor is built into Word, Outlook and Edge: grammar, spelling and basic refinements at no cost, with deeper style checks — clarity, conciseness, formality — for Microsoft 365 subscribers. For anyone inside the Microsoft house, the absence of setup is the feature: it's simply there, in the document, the moment you mistype.

Outside that house it's unremarkable, and its suggestions sit a clear notch below Grammarly's polish on tone and nuance — fine for catching errors, weaker on making prose sing. The advanced checks also ride the Microsoft 365 subscription, so "free" depends on what your employer or household already pays (verify current). Default inside Office; nobody's reason to switch into it.

What we loved

  • Built into Word, Outlook, Edge
  • Zero setup if you're on 365
  • Basic checks genuinely free

What hurt

  • Best parts need Microsoft 365
  • Less polished on tone and nuance
  • Nothing outside the MS ecosystem
Pricing: Free basics · advanced checks with Microsoft 365 (verify current)
8Hemingway7.3

The drill sergeant. Hemingway doesn't negotiate: it highlights long sentences, passive voice, adverbs and readability grade in bright, accusatory colors until your prose does push-ups. It won't catch a subject-verb disagreement, and it doesn't pretend to — what it catches is the bloat that makes readers stop reading. As a discipline tool for drafts that meander, nothing here is more bracing.

The web version is free; the desktop app is a small one-time purchase (reported around $19.99 — verify current). But it's a style coach, not a grammar checker in the proper sense: pair it with a real proofreader rather than replacing one, and ignore it when the flatness it pushes for isn't what the piece needs. Brevity is a virtue; it isn't the whole faith.

Two honest notes. First, the free tiers here are unusually real — Grammarly Free, QuillBot Free, Editor and DeepL Write are working tools, so most people should exhaust free before paying anything. Second, combinations are normal and cheap: a proofreader for correctness (Grammarly or LanguageTool) plus a style tool for discipline (Hemingway or Wordtune) is the common two-tool setup among people who write for a living.

The traps that are the same everywhere

Corrections are suggestions, not verdicts. Every checker here occasionally "fixes" something you meant — a deliberate fragment, a technical term, a brand name. Accepting every underline is how personality dies and how technical accuracy quietly leaks away. You stay the editor; the tool is a very fast, very confident intern.

Privacy is a tier here more than anywhere, because the product reads your words. Free tiers may use submissions to train models, and everything you paste travels to someone's server — so confidential contracts and client material belong in tools whose terms you've actually read, or offline, not in whichever free checker is open.

Annual prices are the real prices. This category advertises the annual-billed monthly number and charges double or triple for month-to-month; promo discounts come and go constantly. Judge cost by the annual figure at checkout, not the banner.

And a checker is not an editor: for anything consequential — a book, a legal letter, a public statement — a human read beats every underline. Prices and tiers above are as of August 2026 and flagged "needs verification" where the vendor page wasn't re-checked.

The verdict

No budget: Grammarly Free or QuillBot Free, with DeepL Write as the free last-pass polish.

Daily professional English: Grammarly Pro, billed annually — the monthly price is not worth meeting.

Multilingual life: LanguageTool; there is no real rival at the job.

A book in you: ProWritingAid, and let its reports be homework.

Student or budget toolkit: QuillBot Premium only after the free tier stops being enough.

Finding your English voice: Wordtune, as the seasoning shelf.

Microsoft household: Editor — already there, already paid for.

Wordy drafts: Hemingway's free web pass, then stop when the flatness shows.

Rico? I set him up with the free tier of the big one and DeepL Write for his client emails, with one rule: the app flags, he decides. His next job ad came back with two suggestions — one accepted, one rejected on the grounds that "it makes me sound like a bank." That's the tool working as intended.

FAQ

Is Grammarly worth paying for in 2026?
If you write at work daily and in English, it's the strongest all-around pick — its corrections plus tone and clarity suggestions are the most polished in the category. The free tier catches the basics honestly, so try that first; Pro runs about $12 a month billed annually (verify current), and the monthly price is much higher.
What is the best free grammar checker?
QuillBot's free tier is the most generous for grammar plus paraphrasing, Grammarly Free is the most polished for everyday corrections, and Microsoft Editor is already inside Word and Edge at no cost. All three cap advanced features behind paywalls — free is a real tool with a ceiling, not a demo.
Do grammar checkers work for languages other than English?
Mostly no — Grammarly and ProWritingAid are English-only. LanguageTool is the exception, checking grammar and style across 25+ languages, which makes it the default for anyone writing in French, German, Spanish or several others. DeepL Write covers a smaller set of languages but with strong quality.
Is my writing private when I use these tools?
Your text goes to the vendor's servers, and terms differ — some free tiers may use submissions for model training. Read the privacy policy of whatever you settle on, and keep confidential contracts or client material in tools whose terms you've checked, or offline entirely.
Can a grammar checker replace a human editor?
No. Checkers catch errors and flag bloat; they don't understand intent, audience or what a piece is for — and they confidently "fix" deliberate choices. For everyday writing that's fine; for a book, a legal letter or anything public, a human read is still the last line.
